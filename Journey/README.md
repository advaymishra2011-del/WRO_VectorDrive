Imagine the look of surprise on our faces when we walked into the lab and found that our carefully designed Ackermann prints had been replaced by a pair of tiny white Jordans sitting on the print bed.

That was just one of the many unexpected moments in our WRO preparation. Our journey was anything but a straight line. It was filled with failed prints, stubborn hardware, late nights, last-minute fixes, and small victories that made the entire build worth it.

It all started with design. We spent countless late nights in Fusion, iterating through chassis models, tweaking mounts, adjusting tolerances, and redesigning parts that looked perfect on screen but didn't quite fit once they were printed. Every change meant re-exporting, re-printing, and testing again. It was slow and frustrating at times, but it taught us one of the most important lessons of the build: designing for the real world is very different from designing on a screen.

Then came the hardware problems. Our BNO sensor gave us inconsistent I2C readings, with addresses sometimes refusing to appear as expected. We rewired, retested, scanned, and debugged until we finally got stable data. Even our PCB testing turned into a mystery: while checking the circuit with a multimeter, the DRV motor driver showed a faint, ghostly green light despite there being no measurable current where we expected it. Moments like that meant going back to basics, questioning every connection, and testing everything again.

Our Raspberry Pis were another story altogether. The Pi 5 came dangerously close to disaster when a RoboSports friend accidentally connected it to a battery charger, while the Pi 5 fan also ended up getting fried. On top of that, our Pis behaved inconsistently, sometimes working perfectly and sometimes refusing to cooperate for reasons that seemed impossible to find.

And then, just hours before our flight, we discovered that our PCB still wasn't working as expected. With almost no time left, we improvised, using jumper wires directly on the board to bypass faulty connections and keep testing. It wasn't the clean solution we had planned, but it kept the bot alive when we needed it most.

Looking back, WRO preparation wasn't really about building a perfect robot. It was about learning how to keep going when nothing works the way you expect. From stolen print beds and failed tolerances to mysterious sensors, glowing motor drivers, unreliable Pis, fried hardware, and last-minute fixes, every problem became part of the story.

Whether or not our bot outperforms everyone else, it represents a month of intense focus, problem-solving, teamwork, and application. We gave it everything we had, and somewhere between the failed prints, late nights, and frantic fixes, we built more than a robot. We built an experience we'll never forget.
